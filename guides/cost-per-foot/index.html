Christmas light cost per foot, and why we do not use it

"How much per foot?" is the most common question we get about price. Here is the honest answer, and what we use instead.

Why do people ask about cost per foot?

Because it feels like the fair way to compare two quotes. You measure your roofline, multiply by a rate, and you have a number you can hold both companies to. It is the same instinct that makes people ask for a price per square foot on flooring or concrete.

It is a reasonable instinct. The problem is that a roofline is not a floor. A floor is flat and every square foot of it takes about the same amount of work. A roofline is not, and that is where a per-foot rate quietly falls apart.

What a per-foot rate leaves out

Picture two houses with exactly 120 feet of front roofline.

The first is a single-story ranch. One straight run, a shallow pitch, the gutter is reachable from a six-foot ladder, and a crew can work the whole front from the driveway. That is a morning.

The second is a two-story with three separate roof planes, a gable over the entry, a balcony, and one section you can only reach by setting a tall ladder on a slope. Same 120 feet. It is a full day, and it takes two people instead of one for most of it.

If both houses are quoted at the same rate per foot, one of those jobs is being subsidised by the other. Companies that quote per foot know this, which is why the per-foot number usually comes with a list of adjustments attached — a story multiplier, a pitch multiplier, a minimum charge. At that point you no longer have a simple rate. You have a formula with the complicated parts hidden inside it.

The bigger problem: what is the rate buying?

Say you find a real per-foot number online. It is almost never clear what it includes. Sometimes it covers hanging only, and you buy the lights. Sometimes the lights are rented separately. Sometimes takedown is a second invoice in January. Sometimes a service call to replace a dead bulb is billable.

That is how a cheap-sounding number turns into a more expensive season. The hanging was never the whole cost. The strands, the timers, the clips, the repairs and the takedown are the cost, and a per-foot rate that quotes only the hanging is measuring the smallest part of the job.

What we do instead

We publish two packages with real ranges, and the range is everything.

Basic Roofline runs about $400–$800 for most homes. That is C9 LED lights along your roofline, timers, roof-safe clips, the install, every repair during the season, and takedown and storage in January.

Premium Full Front runs about $1,000–$2,500 for most homes. Same list, plus the entry and the garage, trees and bushes wrapped, pathway and railing lights, and custom colours.

Then we look at your actual house and tell you where in the range it lands. Not a rate multiplied by a measurement — one number for the job in front of us.

So what decides where my house lands?

Five things, in roughly the order they matter:

  • How much roofline you want lit. More feet is more time and more material. This part really is linear, which is the grain of truth inside the per-foot question.
  • How many stories. Height changes the ladder work, the safety setup, and how many people have to be on the job at once.
  • How much beyond the roof. Wrapping a mature tree properly is slow, careful work. So are pathway runs and railings. This is most of the difference between the two packages.
  • Design complexity. One warm-white run is simple. Alternating colours, a custom mix, or a pattern that has to line up across two roof planes takes planning and more cutting.
  • Anything unusual. Tile roof, steep pitch, no safe ladder footing, power a long way from where the lights go.

How do I compare quotes, then?

Ignore the rate and compare four things instead. Who owns the lights at the end of the season? Is takedown included in the number you were quoted? Are in-season repairs free or billable? And is the quote for the whole display you actually want, or for the roofline with the trees added later?

Two quotes that answer those four questions the same way are genuinely comparable. Two per-foot rates usually are not.
